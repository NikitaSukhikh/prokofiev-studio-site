03 / About

A living cultural organism.

Prokofiev Studio is a creative space in the heart of Hackney, dedicated to preserving and reimagining the artistic archive of the Prokofiev family.

Rooted in a remarkable archive while embracing contemporary practice, the studio serves as a place for visual art, music, performance and interdisciplinary dialogue.

At its core is an important collection of works by artist Oleg Prokofiev, whose paintings, sculptures and works on paper form the foundation of the programme. Alongside this visual heritage is an active musical environment shaped by composer Gabriel Prokofiev.

Historical archives inspire new ideas as artists, musicians, writers and audiences come together through exhibitions, concerts, talks, workshops and experimental projects.

The programme is curated by Anzhela Popova, bringing archival research, contemporary practice and international cultural dialogue into conversation.

Solo exhibition by Oleg Prokofiev

Beyond the Line

July — September 2026 · Curated by Anzhela Popova

Solo Exhibition by Oleg Prokofiev
Curated by Anzhela Popova
Prokofiev Studio
13 Ramsgate Street, London E8 2FD, United Kingdom

Beyond the Line explores a decisive moment in Oleg Prokofiev's artistic evolution, when drawing, painting and sculpture began to dissolve into one another through an increasingly radical exploration of line, colour and space.

The exhibition focuses on works created after Prokofiev's first visit to the United States in 1977–78. His encounter with New York profoundly transformed his artistic language. The city's overwhelming verticality, restless energy and architectural rhythm inspired a remarkable series of drawings titled Stripes, while visits to the Museum of Modern Art, the Guggenheim Museum and the Metropolitan Museum exposed him to abstract painting on an unprecedented scale. The works of Mark Rothko, Clyfford Still, Sol LeWitt, Robert Motherwell, Barnett Newman and Helen Frankenthaler encouraged a new confidence in reduction, colour and formal clarity.

After returning, Oleg Prokofiev developed a visual language in which colour became an active structural force rather than a descriptive element. He created compositions built from contrasting bands, rhythmic intervals and shifting tonal relationships. These works move beyond conventional distinctions between painting and drawing, treating line as both gesture and architecture, capable of constructing space rather than merely describing it.

This search naturally extended into sculpture. During the late 1970s and early 1980s, wood became Prokofiev's principal medium for investigating form in three dimensions. Rather than treating sculpture as static mass, Prokofiev conceived it as an orchestration of movement, balance and void. Influenced by Constructivist principles and resonating with the spatial experiments of Alexander Calder, his reliefs and freestanding sculptures establish a dialogue between weight and lightness, enclosure and openness, presence and absence. Empty space becomes as significant as the material itself, while colour activates the sculptural surface as an integral component of form.

Underlying these works is a deeply poetic understanding of abstraction. Echoing Paul Klee's belief that artistic creation begins from an invisible point from which all forms emerge, Prokofiev sought to distance himself from literal representation in favour of a more universal visual language. His sculptures do not imitate nature; they generate their own systems of rhythm, growth and transformation. Their forms remain open, unfinished and continually reconfigured through the viewer's movement around them.

Beyond the Line reveals this pivotal transition in Prokofiev's practice, where line becomes structure, colour becomes space and sculpture becomes a living choreography of balance and motion. The exhibition presents an artist who continuously expanded the possibilities of abstraction, transforming geometric order into an expressive, lyrical and profoundly human language.

Yield

12—20 June 2026 · Curated by Caroline McCambridge and Aileen Kelly

Prokofiev Studio
13 Ramsgate Street, London E8 2FD, United Kingdom

Yield explores what it means to bend, adapt, accommodate or give way in response to the pressures around us. Through a range of materials and approaches, the exhibition reflects on how tension, resilience, fragility, and change shape our everyday lives.

“Life doesn’t last; art doesn’t last.” Eva Hesse

The works consider how we move through spaces and systems and how bodies and materials respond to forces such as weight, stress, contact and support. Some works appear stretched, softened, balanced or under pressure; others suggest moments of adjustment, resistance or release.

Rather than seeing yielding as weakness or surrender, the exhibition approaches it as a form of responsiveness, a way of remaining open and adaptable within changing conditions. Echoing Donna Haraway’s invitation to “stay with the trouble”, the exhibition pays attention to the uncertain, shifting spaces where transformation can occur.

Oleg Prokofiev · Valentino Vannini · Kirill Basalaev

Bending Time

1—29 May 2026 · Curated by Anzhela Popova

Group Exhibition
Oleg Prokofiev, Valentino Vannini, Kirill Basalaev
Curated by Anzhela Popova
Prokofiev Studio
13 Ramsgate Street, London E8 2FD, United Kingdom

The inaugural exhibition Bending Time at Prokofiev Studio is conceived as a group exhibition structured around the notion of the archive – understood not as a static repository, but as a living, generative system through which artistic legacies are continuously reactivated, reconfigured, and brought into the present. Rather than preserving the past, the archive here operates as a dynamic field of relations, where memory, material, and form remain in a state of transformation.

At the core of the exhibition is a reconstruction of Oleg Prokofiev’s studio from the 1990s, originally located on Dace Road in Hackney Wick. This environment is an active spatial framework that reveals the conditions of the artist’s thinking. Within it, a selection of Prokofiev’s wooden sculptures is presented in close proximity to their original context, allowing the viewer to encounter them not as isolated objects, but as part of an ongoing process.

Prokofiev’s works, developed following his experience of New York in 1977–78, are grounded in the principle he described as the “line in space.” Wooden strips condense into knots or container-like forms, extending into chains, garlands, or serpentine structures. Like the “space-modulations” described by Eduardo Chillida, these works do not simply occupy space but actively produce it. The line – thick, tangible, and materially dense – moves freely, yet is bound by a single rule: it must always return to its point of origin. This cyclical movement establishes a crucial conceptual link to the exhibition as a whole, where time, memory, and form are understood as recursive rather than linear.

Despite their physical density, these sculptures generate a powerful sense of internal space. It is the tension between mass and void, containment and expansion, that defines their presence. The works continually reorganise themselves in relation to their surroundings, extending beyond objecthood toward installation, where space itself becomes sculptural. In this way, Prokofiev’s practice establishes a foundational logic for the exhibition: form as a site of negotiation between structure and instability, presence and absence.

This logic extends into the archival materials preserved within the reconstructed studio – documents, manuscripts, photographs, sketchbooks, and ephemera. These elements are not presented as historical evidence, but as active components of the exhibition. As carriers of time, they mediate between past and present, revealing the archive as a process rather than a conclusion. Fragmented and incomplete, they mirror the very structure of memory itself, reinforcing the idea that meaning is continuously produced through reactivation.

The main gallery expands this framework through the work of Valentino Vannini, and Kirill Basalaev. Their practices do not follow Prokofiev’s legacy directly, but resonate with it through shared concerns with materiality, temporality, and transformation. Together, they form a network of affinities in which the archive becomes distributed across bodies, materials, and spatial conditions.

As proposed by curator Anzhela Popova, the exhibition operates through a “return to the future” – a movement that uncovers unrealised potentials of the past while foregrounding possibilities embedded in the present. Within this context, memory is understood not as preservation, but as an active, destabilising force that reshapes perception.

For Kirill Basalaev, cement – typically associated with permanence – becomes a fragile and unstable carrier of memory. His works appear as archaeological imprints: traces of forms that never fully materialised, yet persist as residues of presence. These forms exist outside linear time, suggesting that matter itself holds the capacity to remember. Cracks, scratches, and erosion – drawn from urban surfaces – become metaphors for memory as fragmented and layered. Through this material language, Basalaev extends the exhibition’s central tension between construction and collapse, revealing memory as both a site of loss and a condition for new perception.

Valentino Vannini’s practice shifts this inquiry toward the immediacy of transformation. If Basalaev’s works hold memory as traces, Vannini’s materials perform change in real time. His multidisciplinary approach creates a sensual field in which materiality resists stability. Reinforced concrete, petroleum jelly, wax, resin, bitumen, fibres, and glass are combined in ways that foreground contradiction: rigid and soft, viscous and solid, enduring and fragile.

These materials echo architectural forms – frameworks, scaffolding, enclosures – but destabilise their logic. Structures sag, stretch, or dissolve into organic configurations, exposing the vulnerability inherent in systems of control. In this way, Vannini’s work enters into dialogue with Prokofiev’s spatial constructions by pushing their internal tensions toward collapse and transformation.

Across these practices, a shared condition emerges: connection is never fixed, but always in flux. Each encounter – between material and form, past and present, body and space – produces something new, shaped as much by absence as by presence. Like Prokofiev’s line returning to its origin, these relations are cyclical, recursive, and continually redefined.

Bending Time thus unfolds as a field of correspondences in which archive, memory, and material are inseparable. What is presented is not a stable narrative, but a shifting constellation of forms and relations – where the past is not recovered, but reactivated, and where meaning remains open, contingent, and in motion.

Solo exhibition by Oleg Prokofiev

Composing Space

13—27 February 2026 · Curated by Anzhela Popova

Solo Exhibition by Oleg Prokofiev
Curated by Anzhela Popova
Prokofiev Studio
13 Ramsgate Street, London E8 2FD, United Kingdom
www.olegprokofiev.com

The contemporary art world has historically revolved around three major capitals — New York, London, and Paris. Following the Second World War, however, the balance of artistic power shifted: Paris gradually lost its leading position, while New York began to rise rapidly. This transformation was driven by the influx of artists, collectors, and critics, alongside the emergence of new artistic languages, most notably Abstract Expressionism developed in New York in the late 1940s and 1950s, positioning the city as the birthplace of the first internationally influential American art movement. It was in New York that contemporary art found not only an aesthetic foundation but also a robust market framework.

In the post-war period, particularly from the 1950s through the 1960s, New York became a site for the establishment of key institutions and influential figures. Museums of global significance, including MoMA, founded earlier in 1929 but greatly expanded in influence after 1945, played a crucial role in shaping the narrative of modern and contemporary art. At the same time, the role of galleries and art dealers grew increasingly prominent. As a result, by the mid-twentieth century, New York had firmly established itself as one of the principal centres of contemporary art and the global art market.

This exhibition explores how the city shaped the artistic development of Oleg Prokofiev during the 1970s. At the end of 1971, Oleg moved from Moscow, USSR, to London, England. He was awarded a Gregory Fellowship in painting at the University of Leeds, which provided him with the opportunity to become a full-time artist for the first time in his life. Both the academic environment and the broader artistic milieu of London played a transformative role in shaping his practice.

In 1977, Oleg travelled briefly to New York, a visit that proved pivotal in the development of his work. Upon his return, he immediately embarked on a series of Manhattan drawings, titled Stripes. The overwhelming scale of the city — fundamentally non-European in character, defined by its aggressive verticality — fascinated him deeply. The architecture and excitement of New York City were not the only experiences to be had. There were also the MOMA, the Guggenheim, the Metropolitan – all showing contemporary work, especially abstract work, which Oleg had not had a chance to absorb in such impressive displays before: Rothko; Still; Sol de Witt; Motherwell; Barnett Newman; Helen Frankenthaler – all no doubt had their influence and resulted in the reductive, painterly abstraction that culminated in the stripe paintings. In translating these impressions into painting, Oleg began to experiment with stronger, and at times deliberately clashing, colours, leading to an entirely new approach to both painting and drawing. During this period, he produced a series of large and medium-scale works in gouache and acrylic, exploring previously unknown intensities and combinations of colour. These drawings were guided by contrasts, dominated by variations of line and tone. Colour became a key instrument in breaking traditional boundaries between painting, drawing, and sculpture.

This initiated a period of inventive experimentation and exploration. Oleg began working with three-dimensional objects, incorporating a variety of materials — wood, fragments of metal, and objects often found on the street. His practice shifted from painting and drawing towards relief and sculpture. He described this approach as the principle of “line in space”. By joining cut-out elements from a board, the line could move in any direction, yet one strict rule applied: it must always return to its point of origin. The “line” itself is thick and tangible, most often rectangular or square in section. Despite this material density, the sculptures possess a strong sense of condensed space. It is the dynamic interplay between this internal space and the surrounding environment that ultimately defines the sculptural form as a whole.

Oleg’s abstract drawings and sculptures operate according to a logic akin to musical composition, where rhythm emerges from a balance between order and controlled disruption. Like a composer structuring a work to occupy a predetermined duration, carefully calibrating sound and silence, Prokofiev organises space through deliberate modulation. A finite spatial field is articulated through varying densities of form, constructed from interrelated contrasts of stripes, angles, curves, and loops. Dynamics and pauses are integral to this construction, activating the space and guiding the viewer’s perception through moments of tension and release.

Poetry evening

Poetry Evening: Oleg Prokofiev

8 July 2026 · Read by Gabriel Prokofiev & Maria Bregman

Prokofiev Studio
13 Ramsgate Street, London E8 2FD, United Kingdom

An evening of poetry by Oleg Prokofiev. Read by Gabriel Prokofiev & Maria Bregman.

Join us for an evening dedicated to the poetry of Oleg Prokofiev, offering a rare opportunity to experience his literary voice alongside his artistic legacy. The readings will be presented by Gabriel Prokofiev, composer and son of Oleg Prokofiev, and Maria Bregman, poet, literary critic and writer, bringing together personal insight and performative interpretation.

The event creates a dialogue between Prokofiev’s poetic works, inviting audiences to reflect on memory, materiality, and the passage of time.

Oleg Prokofiev was a significant yet largely undiscovered poet whose work is only now beginning to receive the attention it deserves. At the time of his death in 1998, he left behind several hundred poems, all written in Russian and preserved within his family archive.

Prokofiev’s poetic language is imagistic and fragmentary, often resisting translation. Dispensing with punctuation, he relies on line breaks to shape meaning, creating a syntax that is at once fluid and ambiguous. His work frequently gestures toward pre-verbal and supra-verbal states of perception, with imagery that is both open-ended and hermetic.

Documentary screening by Maria Bregman

I Choose to Live

23 April 2026 · Duration 49 min

Screening of a documentary by Maria Bregman
Lydia Grigorieva. I Choose to Live
Prokofiev Studio
13 Ramsgate Street, London E8 2FD, United Kingdom

The film opens with scenes of Lydia Grigorieva’s London home and garden, gradually transporting the viewer to memories of her childhood spent in the Far North. Lydia reads her own poetry, while archival footage of the 1960s “Thaw” era evokes the atmosphere of her youth, her first steps in poetry, and the circle of young writers she belonged to — among them Joseph Brodsky, Yevgeny Rein, and Dmitry Bobyshev.

The film is dedicated to the 80th anniversary of the renowned poet. Together with her, we embark on a journey through personal losses — the deaths of loved ones, the weight of private tragedies. Yet even in these moments, Lydia remains resilient: her poetry and inner world continue to flourish. We follow her through the garden, witnessing its lush bloom, and begin to perceive the richness of her inner life, where the earthly and the eternal intertwine.

Lydia Nikolaevna Grigorieva is a poet, prose writer, and photographic artist. She graduated from the Faculty of Philology at Kazan University. In the 1980s she was the author and host of the radio programmes Poetic Encounters and The Poet’s Studio on All-Union Radio. Since 1992 she has lived both in London and Moscow. She is the author of television films about the time Marina Tsvetaeva, Nikolai Gumilev, and Alexander Scriabin spent in London, as well as poetry collections, verse novels such as Circle of Acquaintances and The Russian Wife of an English Gentleman, and prose and essays including England — The Country of Councils, Earthly and Heavenly Gardens, and the three-volume Novel in Strokes: The Termite Mound 1, 2, 3.

She is the creator of the synthetic genres “Photopoetry” and “Cinepoem.” A member of the Union of Writers of the USSR, the European Society of Culture, and PEN International, she is the recipient of the WOW Award 2025 “For the fire of the Russian word preserved in every line and shared with the world.”
